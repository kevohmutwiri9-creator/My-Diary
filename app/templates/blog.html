Journaling: Practical Exercises and Evidence

Short, optional exercises with research links and clear limits

About this page

This is educational material, not medical or mental-health advice. Research findings vary by study and person. You do not need to write about painful experiences; stop if an exercise makes you feel worse and seek qualified support when appropriate.

A short expressive-writing exercise

Expressive-writing research studies ask participants to write about thoughts and feelings connected with an experience. The studies do not establish that one exercise is right for everyone, or that writing replaces treatment. A review of this research reports varying effects across outcomes and study conditions.

A lower-pressure version to try

  1. Choose an everyday event that feels manageable. Avoid a topic you do not want to revisit.
  2. Write for five minutes about what happened and what you noticed.
  3. End by noting what would help you now, or leave the entry unfinished.
  4. Decide whether you want to keep, edit, or delete what you wrote.

Pause if needed: You can stop, switch to a neutral subject, or speak with someone you trust. There is no need to push through distress.

Make a gratitude entry specific

Gratitude exercises have been studied in relation to subjective well-being. In one experiment, participants recorded blessings or hassles; results from that particular study are not a guarantee of effects for other people or settings.

Try a three-line entry

  1. Name one ordinary thing you appreciated today.
  2. Describe what made it meaningful to you, if anything.
  3. Include a difficulty too if that better reflects your day; you do not have to force positivity.

Examples can be small and concrete: a helpful message, a quiet moment, or finishing a task. If this exercise feels uncomfortable, choose another prompt.

Build a routine that can bend

A journaling habit does not need a streak, a particular notebook, or a daily word count. A flexible cue can make it easier to remember while leaving room for missed days.

  • Choose a cue: after lunch, before bed, or when you close your laptop.
  • Set a small default: one sentence or three minutes is enough to begin.
  • Keep a fallback prompt: “What do I want to remember about today?”
  • Review gently: look for questions or themes, not proof that you should feel a certain way.
  • Restart without catching up: after a gap, write about today rather than filling missed pages.

Sources and evidence limits

These papers are starting points for reading the research. They study particular activities and populations; their results should not be generalized into claims that journaling prevents or treats health conditions.

  1. Smyth, J. M. (1998). Written emotional expression: effect sizes, outcome types, and moderating variables. Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology, 66(1), 174–184.
  2. Emmons, R. A., & McCullough, M. E. (2003). Counting blessings versus burdens: an experimental investigation of gratitude and subjective well-being in daily life. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 84(2), 377–389.

If writing brings up persistent distress, stop and consider talking with a qualified health professional. This site does not provide crisis support.
