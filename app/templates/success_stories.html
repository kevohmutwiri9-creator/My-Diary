Journaling Practice Examples

Short exercises you can adapt to your own experience

These are fictional practice examples, not real user stories, testimonials, or promises of results. Journaling affects people differently and is not a substitute for medical or mental-health care.

1. Notice a recurring situation

Suppose a person often feels tense before a weekly meeting. Instead of assuming the cause, they could record what happened, their feeling, and any relevant context for a few weeks.

Try these prompts:

  • What happened just before I noticed the feeling?
  • What was I thinking, and what else was going on that day?
  • Is there a pattern in these notes, or is more time needed?

A journal can help organize personal observations; it cannot establish a diagnosis or prove why a feeling occurred.

2. Turn a broad goal into one next step

“Get better at my craft” is difficult to act on. A useful entry can make the goal smaller and easier to revisit without treating a missed day as failure.

  1. Write what you would like to make progress on.
  2. Choose an action that can be done in 15 minutes or less.
  3. Note when or where you might do it.
  4. At the next entry, record what helped or got in the way.

Example: “I want to practice drawing. On Thursday after lunch, I’ll sketch one object for 10 minutes. If I miss it, I’ll choose another time rather than abandon the goal.”

3. Write about a difficult day with care

Emotional writing is not right for every person or every moment. If writing feels overwhelming, stop, switch to a neutral topic, or talk with someone you trust. You do not need to revisit painful experiences to keep a journal.

  1. Set a short time limit and choose a topic that feels manageable.
  2. Describe what you noticed without judging yourself for feeling it.
  3. Finish by naming one thing you need now, such as rest, company, or practical help.

What research can—and cannot—tell us

Studies of expressive writing and gratitude exercises have reported varied outcomes. Results depend on the people studied, the writing activity, and the outcome measured. They do not show that journaling treats a mental-health condition or works the same way for everyone.

  • Smyth, J. M. (1998). Written emotional expression: effect sizes, outcome types, and moderating variables. Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology.
  • Emmons, R. A., & McCullough, M. E. (2003). Counting blessings versus burdens: an experimental investigation of gratitude and subjective well-being in daily life. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology.

Use these papers as starting points, not as a guarantee of personal benefit. If you have ongoing distress or concerns about your health, contact a qualified professional.
