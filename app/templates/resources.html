Journaling Resources & Tips

Practical prompts and references for a flexible journaling practice

Getting Started with Journaling

Your First Entry

Starting a journal can feel overwhelming. Here's how to begin:

  1. Set the Scene: Find a quiet, comfortable space
  2. Start Small: Write just 5-10 minutes initially
  3. Be Honest: Write without judgment or censorship
  4. Don't Worry: Forget grammar, spelling, or structure
  5. Be Consistent: Try to write at the same time daily

Remember: Write only what you are comfortable keeping in a journal. You can skip any prompt or stop at any time.

Building the Habit

Consistency is key to successful journaling:

  • Habit Stacking: Link journaling to existing habits (morning coffee, bedtime)
  • Set Reminders: Use phone alerts or calendar notifications
  • Prepare Your Space: Keep your journal and pen accessible
  • Start a Streak: Use a calendar to track consecutive days
  • Forgive Yourself: Missed a day? Just start again tomorrow

There is no fixed number of days that works for everyone. Start small and return when it suits you; a missed day does not undo your practice.

Effective Journaling Techniques

Bullet Journaling

Use bullet points to capture thoughts, tasks, and events when a short list suits you.

Example:
  • • Meeting with boss went well
  • • Feeling anxious about presentation
  • • Grateful for supportive coworker
Gratitude Journaling

Focus on things you're thankful for. Shifts mindset to positivity and abundance.

Example:
  • • Morning coffee tasted perfect
  • • Friend called to check in
  • • Found parking spot immediately
Mood Tracking

Record a mood in your own words if useful. Notes may help you recall what you experienced, but they cannot explain its cause or assess your mental health.

Example:
  • • Morning: Anxious (6/10)
  • • Afternoon: Calm (3/10)
  • • Evening: Happy (2/10)

Journaling Prompts

Self-Reflection

  • What am I most proud of this week?
  • When did I feel most alive today?
  • What limiting belief am I ready to release?
  • How have I grown in the past year?
  • What would my younger self be proud of?

Goals & Dreams

  • What would I do if I knew I couldn't fail?
  • What's one small step toward my big goal?
  • Who inspires me and why?
  • What does success look like to me?
  • What am I procrastinating on and why?

Relationships

  • Who made me feel loved today?
  • How can I be a better friend/partner?
  • Is there a conversation I want to have, and what would I like to say?
  • Who am I grateful for in my life?
  • How do I show love to others?

Daily Life

  • What was the best part of my day?
  • What challenged me today?
  • What did I learn today?
  • What made me laugh today?
  • How did I take care of myself today?

Reflection and wellbeing

What a journal can—and cannot—do

A journal is a place to record your own words and observations. It cannot determine why you feel a certain way, diagnose a condition, or replace professional advice. Research on structured writing exercises studies particular activities and populations; results do not guarantee that a prompt will help any individual.

Record an event

Note what happened, when it happened, and details you want to remember. Keep observations separate from interpretations when that distinction is useful.

Explore a question

Write down what you know, what you are unsure about, and whether there is a practical next step. It is also fine to leave a question unanswered.

Keep a project note

Record a task, what you tried, and what you might do next. Notes can help you remember your own decisions; they cannot guarantee a result.

Choose your boundaries

You do not need to write about painful experiences. Skip a prompt, switch to an ordinary topic, or stop if writing feels distressing. Consider speaking with someone you trust or a qualified professional if you need support.

Flexible writing options

  • Write about an ordinary event
  • Use a list instead of paragraphs
  • Include mixed feelings if you want
  • Choose how often to review entries
  • Skip days without catching up
  • Stop if a prompt is uncomfortable
  • Leave a question open
  • Use a prompt only when helpful

Advanced Journaling Topics

Dream Journaling

If you want to remember a dream, jot down what you recall and how you felt on waking. Notes are personal recollections; symbols do not have a fixed meaning, and a dream entry cannot reveal a hidden diagnosis or truth.

Tips:
  • Keep notebook by your bed
  • Record details immediately
  • Note emotions in dreams
  • Note details without assuming what they mean

Relationship Mapping

Explore your connections with others through journaling. Analyze communication patterns, boundaries, and emotional needs.

Focus Areas:
  • Communication styles
  • Emotional triggers
  • Support systems
  • Conflict resolution

Career Journaling

Record project details, skills you are practicing, and questions to revisit when preparing for a performance review or job search.

Include:
  • Project accomplishments
  • Skills development
  • Networking contacts
  • Career goals

Creative Journaling

Combine writing with art, photos, or other materials if a visual format suits your practice.

Try:
  • Doodle and sketch
  • Add photos and mementos
  • Use colored pens
  • Create mind maps
